Why Don't I Feel Like
Myself After a Concussion?

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍️ Dave Dyck, MC, CCC 📍 Nanaimo, BC

If your family says you've changed, or you don't recognize yourself in the mirror anymore, you're describing something real. Here's why a concussion can change who you feel like, and what helps you find your way back.

I often hear people say some version of the same thing after a concussion: "I'm not me anymore." Sometimes it's their family who says it first — a partner mentioning, gently or not so gently, that they seem different. More irritable. More emotional. Quieter, or somehow harder to reach.

It can feel confusing to hear that from someone who loves you, especially when you don't fully recognize the change yourself, or when you do recognize it and don't know what to do about it. Many people wonder if the person they were before the accident is simply gone. I want you to know, as clearly as I can say it: what you're describing is real, it's common after a concussion, and it doesn't mean you're gone. It means your brain is recovering from an injury, and recovery changes how things feel along the way.

"I Don't Recognize Myself" Is a Real Description, Not an Exaggeration

When people describe feeling like a different person after a concussion, they're not being dramatic. A concussion is a brain injury, and the brain is the organ responsible for mood, personality expression, memory, and how you process the world around you. When it's injured, even mildly, the ripple effects can touch almost every part of daily life — long before anyone notices a visible symptom.

They tell me things like: "I'm more emotional than I used to be." "I'm irritated all the time, over nothing." "I can't think the way I used to." "I don't enjoy the things I used to enjoy." "I don't recognize myself in the mirror some days." Each of these is a genuine, recognized part of concussion recovery — not a character change, and not something to be ashamed of.

The Grief of Losing Your Old Self, Even Temporarily

There's a kind of grief that comes with this that doesn't get talked about enough. You haven't lost a person the way you would through death, but you have, in a real sense, lost access to a version of yourself — the one who could concentrate for hours, who had more patience, who felt like themselves without having to think about it.

That loss deserves to be grieved, even while you're actively working toward getting that version of yourself back. Change is possible, and for the great majority of people, concussion symptoms do improve significantly with time and the right support. But pretending the in-between period isn't hard, or that you should just push through it, usually makes things worse rather than better.

Why Brain Fatigue Changes Everything

One of the most under-discussed parts of concussion recovery is brain fatigue — a kind of mental exhaustion that's different from ordinary tiredness. It can show up as:

  • Mental overload — feeling overwhelmed by tasks that used to be simple, like following a conversation in a noisy room.
  • Sensitivity to light and noise — a grocery store or a busy family dinner can feel unexpectedly draining or even painful.
  • Decision fatigue — small choices, like what to make for dinner, feeling disproportionately hard.
  • Memory difficulties — losing track of conversations, forgetting why you walked into a room, or struggling to hold information.

Brain fatigue isn't laziness, and it isn't "in your head" in the dismissive sense. It's your brain telling you it has less capacity available right now, and that pushing past it usually sets recovery back rather than moving it forward.

Why You're More Emotional, and More Irritated, Than Usual

Many people are caught off guard by how much more emotionally reactive they feel after a concussion — tearing up more easily, snapping at people they love, feeling a flash of anger over something minor. There's a real physiological reason for this. The brain regions involved in regulating emotion are often affected by concussion, on top of the exhaustion of simply trying to function with reduced capacity.

Your brain isn't wrong to feel this way, and this isn't the "real you" showing through — it's an injured brain doing its best with fewer resources than usual. Understanding this distinction is often one of the most relieving parts of concussion recovery work, both for the person who was injured and for the people around them.

You don't know what you don't know, and most of us were never taught that a concussion can change mood and personality expression just as much as it affects memory or concentration. Nothing is wrong with you for feeling different. Your brain is healing.

When Concussion Recovery and Trauma Overlap

Not every concussion causes trauma, and not everyone who's had a concussion develops anxiety or depression as a result — but for many people who were injured in a frightening event like a car accident, the two are closely linked. The same accident that caused the concussion may also have left a nervous system on high alert, and it can be hard to untangle what's coming from the brain injury itself and what's coming from the trauma of the event that caused it.

This is part of why I approach concussion recovery through a trauma-informed lens rather than treating it as a purely cognitive issue. If anxiety has become part of the picture alongside your concussion symptoms, my article on concussion anxiety and emotional overwhelm goes deeper into how these two things interact.

The Pressure to Recover Quickly

Concussions are sometimes called an "invisible injury," and that invisibility comes with a particular kind of pressure. Because nothing looks broken from the outside, there's often an unspoken expectation — from work, from family, sometimes from yourself — that you should be back to normal faster than your brain is actually able to manage.

This pressure shows up in specific, painful ways: returning to work before you're ready and struggling to keep up, feeling shame about needing accommodations, or feeling misunderstood when people say you "look fine" and assume that means you are fine. Self-worth can take a real hit here, especially for people who are used to being capable and independent. None of that reflects who you are underneath the injury — it reflects how genuinely hard concussion recovery can be when it isn't visible to the people around you.

Isolation, Relationships, and Feeling Misunderstood

It's common to withdraw during concussion recovery, even from people you're close to. Loud gatherings become exhausting instead of enjoyable. Conversations take more effort. Some people stop reaching out altogether, partly from fatigue and partly from not wanting to explain, again, why they're not quite themselves.

This can put real strain on relationships, particularly when the people around you don't fully understand what's happening in your brain. If this is affecting your family, my article on families emotionally exhausted after an accident speaks directly to what loved ones are often experiencing too — because concussion recovery rarely affects just one person.

What Recovery Actually Involves

Concussion recovery works best as a team effort, and counselling is typically one part of a broader, multidisciplinary plan alongside medical care, physiotherapy, and sometimes vestibular or vision therapy. On the counselling side, the work often includes:

  • Understanding the injury — learning why symptoms like brain fatigue, emotional sensitivity, and memory changes happen, which reduces shame and self-blame.
  • Pacing strategies — learning to work with your current capacity rather than pushing through it, which usually speeds recovery rather than slowing it.
  • Processing the identity shift — making space to grieve what feels lost, while building confidence in your recovery.
  • Addressing overlapping trauma or anxiety — when the injury happened in a frightening event like a car accident.
  • Rebuilding connection — helping the people around you understand what you're experiencing, so you feel less alone in it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I feel like a different person after a concussion?

Concussions can affect mood, energy, and cognitive function, which often changes how people think, feel, and interact with others. This can create a real sense of identity loss, even when it isn't talked about as often as physical symptoms.

Is it normal to feel more emotional or irritable after a concussion?

Yes. Increased emotional sensitivity and irritability are common after a concussion, partly due to brain fatigue and partly due to the stress of coping with new limitations day to day.

Can counselling help with concussion recovery?

Yes. Counselling is often one part of a multidisciplinary concussion recovery plan, helping address the emotional, identity, and relationship impacts alongside medical and physiotherapy care.

Does ICBC cover counselling for concussion recovery?

ICBC commonly funds counselling as part of approved motor vehicle accident claims involving concussion, including support for the emotional and cognitive impacts of the injury.
