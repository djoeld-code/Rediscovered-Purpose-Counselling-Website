Understanding the Four Trauma Responses
Fight, Flight, Freeze, and Fawn

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍️ Dave Dyck, MC, CCC 📍 Nanaimo, BC

Trauma doesn't always look like fear. Sometimes it looks like anger, exhaustion, numbness, or an inability to say no. Here's a clear, compassionate guide to the four trauma responses — and what they mean for your recovery.

Many people believe trauma only happens during a terrifying event — the crash itself, the moment of impact, the seconds where everything felt out of control. But trauma isn't really about the event. It's about what happens in your nervous system afterward.

Often, the nervous system keeps reacting long after the actual danger has passed. It stays braced, scans for threat, or shuts down — not because anything is wrong with you, but because that's exactly what a nervous system is designed to do when it hasn't yet learned that the danger is over. You don't know what you don't know, and most of us were never taught how any of this works.

This guide walks through the four main trauma responses — fight, flight, freeze, and fawn — so you can understand what's happening in your own body, and what actually helps. I work with people across Nanaimo and Vancouver Island through trauma counselling in Nanaimo, and this is often one of the first things we talk about.

What the Autonomic Nervous System Does

Your autonomic nervous system runs in the background of everything you do, constantly scanning your environment for safety or danger — a process sometimes called neuroception. When it detects a threat, it triggers one of several survival responses automatically, faster than conscious thought.

This is a crucial point: trauma responses are not choices. Nobody decides to freeze in a dangerous moment, or to snap at a loved one after a hard week, or to agree to something they didn't want to agree to. These are automatic, protective reactions from a part of the brain that isn't interested in social niceties or long-term consequences — only survival. For a closer look at the physiology behind this, see my article on the nervous system after trauma.

The Fight Response

The fight response prepares your body to confront a threat head-on. After trauma, this can show up as a nervous system that stays braced for confrontation long after the danger has passed.

What it often looks like:

  • Road rage or a much shorter fuse behind the wheel
  • General irritability, especially with people you're close to
  • Defensiveness, even in low-stakes conversations
  • A short temper that feels out of proportion to what triggered it
  • Constant physical tension — a clenched jaw, tight shoulders, a readiness to react

If this pattern feels familiar, especially after a motor vehicle accident, my article on why anger is common after a car accident goes into this in more depth.

The Flight Response

The flight response prepares your body to escape danger. In everyday life after trauma, this rarely looks like literally running away — it looks like constant motion, restlessness, and difficulty sitting still with your own thoughts.

What it often looks like:

  • Always being busy, with little tolerance for downtime
  • Restlessness or a persistent sense of unease
  • Anxiety that shows up as physical energy — a racing heart, fidgeting, tension
  • Difficulty relaxing, even when there's nothing urgent to do
  • Overworking as a way to avoid sitting with uncomfortable feelings
  • Overthinking and perfectionism, where the mind stays in motion even when the body can't

The Freeze Response

The freeze response happens when a threat feels too big to fight or too fast to flee. The body's answer is to shut down — a survival strategy that, in the animal kingdom, can genuinely mean the difference between life and death.

What it often looks like:

  • Feeling numb or emotionally flat
  • Brain fog and difficulty concentrating
  • A sense of shutting down or going through the motions
  • Difficulty making even small decisions
  • Avoiding driving, or avoiding the location of an accident altogether
  • Feeling disconnected — from yourself, from others, or from your surroundings

Freeze is often the least understood of the four responses because it can look like laziness or disinterest from the outside. It isn't. It's a nervous system doing what it believes it needs to do to survive.

The Fawn Response

The fawn response involves trying to keep everyone around you calm and happy as a way of staying safe. It develops especially in situations — past or present — where conflict felt dangerous and appeasing others felt like the safer path.

What it often looks like:

  • Chronic people-pleasing, even at your own expense
  • Difficulty saying no, or feeling guilty when you do
  • Working hard to keep everyone around you happy, sometimes before checking in with your own needs
  • A strong fear of conflict, even minor disagreements

Fawn responses often go unnoticed the longest, because on the surface they can look like being agreeable or easygoing. Underneath, they're frequently rooted in a nervous system that learned safety depended on not rocking the boat.

You Can Move Between These Responses

Most people don't experience just one trauma response. It's common to move between fight, flight, freeze, and fawn depending on the situation, who's involved, and how safe your nervous system feels in the moment. You might fawn with your boss, freeze around conflict at home, and fight when you're behind the wheel. None of that means something is wrong with you — it means your nervous system is trying multiple strategies to keep you safe.

Window of Tolerance and Nervous System Regulation

A helpful concept here is your window of tolerance — the zone where you can think clearly, feel your emotions, and respond to life without becoming overwhelmed or shutting down. Trauma tends to narrow that window, making it easier to get pushed into fight, flight, freeze, or fawn by things that wouldn't have bothered you before.

Nervous system regulation is the process of widening that window back out — teaching your body, gradually and safely, that it's allowed to feel calm again. Safety has to come before processing trauma directly; trying to dive into difficult material before the nervous system feels safe often backfires and can leave people feeling worse, not better.

Why Simply Talking About Trauma Isn't Always Enough

One of the most common misconceptions about trauma recovery is that talking about what happened is enough on its own. For some people it helps. For many others, especially those whose nervous system still feels unsafe, talking about the event can actually re-trigger the very response they're trying to move past.

This is why trauma-informed counselling doesn't start with "tell me what happened." It starts with building safety and stability first — so that when you do process the harder material, your nervous system has the resources to handle it without becoming overwhelmed.

How Counselling Helps You Reconnect With Life

In my work with clients through ICBC counselling in Nanaimo and trauma counselling more broadly, the process generally follows a few key stages:

  • Assessment — understanding your history and how trauma is currently showing up in your body and behaviour.
  • Building safety — establishing a sense of stability before addressing anything distressing.
  • Grounding skills — practical tools to bring your nervous system back into your window of tolerance in the moment.
  • Stabilization — strengthening your capacity to handle stress before deeper processing begins.
  • Trauma-informed processing — working through what happened at a pace your nervous system can actually handle.
  • CBT — gently challenging the unhelpful beliefs trauma often creates, like "I'm not safe" or "I can't trust my own judgment."

Over time, this approach helps clients develop real, lasting confidence and reconnect with the life they had before — rather than staying stuck in survival mode. The Canadian Psychological Association offers helpful general background on how trauma and stress affect mental health if you'd like to read further.

You're Not Broken

If you recognize yourself in any of these four responses, I want you to hear this clearly: these patterns are signs of a nervous system that worked hard to protect you. They are not signs that something is wrong with you, and they are not permanent. Change is possible, with patience, understanding, and the right support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you have more than one trauma response?

Yes. Most people move between fight, flight, freeze, and fawn depending on the situation, who else is involved, and how safe their nervous system feels in that moment.

Is freeze the same as laziness?

No. Freeze is an involuntary nervous system response to overwhelm, not a choice or a character flaw. It can look like avoidance or low motivation from the outside, but underneath it's a protective survival response.

Why can't I stop overthinking?

Overthinking is often part of a flight response. Your mind stays in motion, scanning for solutions or danger, because slowing down doesn't yet feel safe to your nervous system.

Can counselling change trauma responses?

Yes. Trauma-informed counselling, including nervous system regulation and CBT, helps your body learn that it's safe again, which allows these automatic responses to soften over time.

How long does trauma recovery take?

Recovery timelines vary from person to person. Some people notice meaningful shifts within weeks of starting trauma counselling; for others, healing unfolds over a longer, steadier process. There's no single right timeline.
