What to Do Emotionally After a Car Accident

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Dave Dyck, MC, CCC 📍 Nanaimo, BC

You already know what to do about the vehicle, the claim, and the paperwork. This is about the part nobody hands you a checklist for — what to do for yourself, emotionally, in the days and weeks after.

Book a Free Consultation →

After a car accident, most of the "what to do" advice is practical. Report it to ICBC. Get the vehicle assessed. See a doctor. Keep your paperwork. It's useful, and it matters — but it also skips over the part that tends to hit hardest: how you're feeling.

If you're reading this because something feels off since the accident — you're more on edge, more tired, more easily overwhelmed, or just not quite yourself — I want you to know that's not a side issue. It's a normal, expected part of recovering from something your body and mind experienced as a threat, even if the accident itself was "minor" on paper. Your brain isn't wrong for reacting this way.

I'm Dave Dyck, a Canadian Certified Counsellor in Nanaimo, and I work with people across Vancouver Island through ICBC counselling in Nanaimo, often in the weeks right after a crash. Here's what I'd want you to know about the emotional side of recovery — what's normal, what actually helps, and when it's worth reaching out for support.

Why the Emotional Side Gets Overlooked

Car accidents move fast, but the aftermath moves slowly and in a very practical direction. There are calls to make, forms to fill out, appointments to book. It's easy — and honestly kind of a relief — to pour your energy into the logistics, because logistics feel solvable. You can check them off a list.

Your emotional response doesn't work like that. It doesn't show up neatly, and it rarely waits for a convenient time. Someone might feel completely fine the day of the accident and then find themselves crying in a parking lot two weeks later for what seems like no reason. Someone else might feel calm behind the wheel and then discover they've started avoiding a certain intersection without even deciding to. None of that means something is wrong with you. It usually means your nervous system is still processing what happened, on its own timeline.

In the First Few Days: What Your Nervous System Needs

Immediately after an accident, adrenaline and shock can mask how much it actually affected you. People often describe feeling strangely calm or functional in the first 24 to 48 hours, only to feel the impact land a few days later. A few things genuinely help in this early window:

  • Keep your routine as steady as you can. Regular meals, regular sleep, and familiar surroundings give your nervous system something predictable to hold onto while it settles.
  • Avoid big decisions in the first few days if you can. Shock affects judgment. Where possible, let urgent-but-not-immediate decisions wait a little.
  • Let your body move, gently. A short walk, some stretching, anything that isn't forced. Movement helps discharge some of the physical activation that comes with a threat response — as long as pain or injury doesn't make that unsafe.
  • Don't isolate completely. You don't need to talk about the accident in detail if you're not ready, but being around a person you trust, even quietly, helps regulate a nervous system that's on high alert.

If you're also managing a possible concussion, some of this changes — rest looks different, and screen time and stimulation need to be paced carefully. If that's part of your situation, my page on concussion counselling in Nanaimo goes into that in more detail.

Give Yourself Permission to Not Be Okay

This sounds simple, but it's often the hardest step. A lot of people I work with feel pressure — from themselves more than anyone else — to be "fine" quickly, especially if the accident wasn't severe or nobody was seriously hurt. They minimize it. It could have been worse. Other people have it worse. I should just be grateful.

Gratitude and distress aren't opposites. You can be genuinely relieved you're alive and still be shaken by what happened. Minimizing your own experience doesn't make it resolve faster — it usually just pushes it underground, where it tends to show up later as irritability, anxiety, or exhaustion that doesn't seem to have a clear cause.

You don't need a diagnosis or a severe injury to justify feeling affected by a car accident. Your nervous system doesn't grade the event on a curve.

Common Emotional Reactions After an Accident

What you feel after an accident can vary a lot from person to person, and it can also shift over time. Some of the most common reactions I see in sessions include:

  • Anxiety that comes and goes, sometimes tied to driving or being a passenger, sometimes showing up for no obvious reason at all. Read more about why anxiety can feel constant after an accident.
  • Hypervigilance — feeling unusually alert, jumpy, or unable to relax, especially in the car. This is covered in more depth in Understanding Hypervigilance After a Car Accident.
  • Irritability or anger that feels out of proportion to what's actually happening — snapping at family, feeling a flash of rage in traffic. I wrote about this directly in Why Anger Is Common After a Car Accident.
  • Fear around driving, whether that's avoiding certain routes, gripping the wheel too tightly, or dreading getting back in the car at all — explored further in Why Driving Feels Scary After a Car Accident.
  • Guilt, particularly if someone else was in the vehicle, if you were driving, or if you keep replaying what you could have done differently.
  • Low mood or a flat, numb feeling, which can be just as disruptive as anxiety, even though it's quieter and easier to dismiss.

These reactions can show up together, take turns, or fade and resurface. That unpredictability is one of the most disorienting parts of accident recovery — you can feel like yourself for a few days and then feel completely knocked sideways again.

Practical Emotional First Steps

Alongside the practical claim and medical steps, here are things you can actually do for your emotional recovery, starting now:

  1. Name what you're feeling, even just to yourself. "I'm anxious," "I'm angry," "I'm numb" — putting a word to it takes some of the charge out of it and makes it easier to talk about later.
  2. Talk to someone about the accident, not just the aftermath. Most conversations after a crash are about logistics. Find at least one person — a partner, friend, or counsellor — you can talk to about how it actually felt.
  3. Pay attention to avoidance. Notice if you're starting to avoid driving, certain roads, or even riding as a passenger. Avoidance often feels like relief in the moment but tends to make anxiety worse over time.
  4. Track how you're doing, loosely. You don't need a journal with daily entries — even a general sense of "better this week" or "worse this week" helps you and your care team notice patterns.
  5. Involve your family gently. The people around you are often affected too, and may not know how to support you. My article on families feeling emotionally exhausted after an accident is a good one to share with them.
  6. Give yourself a longer timeline than you expect to need. Emotional recovery rarely follows a straight line, and that's normal, not a setback.

When to Seek Extra Support

Most people start to feel more like themselves within a few weeks as the initial shock settles. But it's worth reaching out for additional support if you notice:

  • Anxiety, fear, or low mood that isn't easing after two to three weeks
  • Avoidance that's expanding — for example, avoiding not just the accident site but driving altogether
  • Sleep that stays disrupted, or exhaustion that doesn't lift with rest
  • Irritability or anger that's affecting your relationships or work
  • Flashbacks, intrusive images of the accident, or a strong startle response
  • A sense that you're "not yourself" and it's not improving

None of these mean something is broken in you. They mean your nervous system is asking for more support than it's currently getting — and that's exactly what trauma-informed counselling is for. How ICBC Counselling Fits In

If your accident happened in British Columbia, you likely have access to counselling support through ICBC, often without any cost to you directly. ICBC provides funding for a set number of counselling sessions for people recovering from a motor vehicle accident, and many registered providers, including our practice, direct bill ICBC so you're not paying out of pocket or waiting on reimbursement.

I'm an ICBC-registered provider offering ICBC counselling in Nanaimo, with in-person sessions locally and virtual sessions available anywhere in BC. Whether your accident happened last week or several months ago, it's not too late to reach out — a lot of people don't realize this support is available until well after the initial claim process is over.

A Quick Story from Construction

Before I trained as a counsellor, I spent years in construction. One thing that trade taught me has stuck with me ever since: not every problem gets solved by working harder at it. Sometimes you need a different tool, a different approach, or someone with a different set of skills to help you see what's actually going on. Emotional recovery after an accident works the same way. Willpower alone doesn't undo a nervous system stuck in survival mode — but the right support can help it settle.

You Don't Have to Have It All Figured Out

You don't need to know exactly what's wrong, have the right words for it, or wait until things feel "bad enough" to reach out. If something feels different since your accident, that's reason enough to talk to someone. Change is possible, even when it doesn't feel that way right now.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do emotionally after a car accident?

Give yourself permission to not be okay, keep your routine as steady as you can, avoid big decisions in the first few days, and talk to someone about what happened rather than carrying it alone. If anxiety, low mood, or fear are still strong after a few weeks, it's worth reaching out to a counsellor.

Is it normal to feel anxious weeks after a car accident?

Yes. It's common for anxiety to peak two to six weeks after an accident rather than right away, as the initial shock wears off and your nervous system starts processing what happened. The Anxiety Canada website has helpful general information on how anxiety develops after distressing events.

Does ICBC pay for counselling after an accident?

Yes. ICBC covers a set number of counselling sessions for people recovering from a motor vehicle accident in BC, and many registered providers direct bill ICBC directly, so you don't pay out of pocket. You can find more detail on what ICBC covers here.

How long does emotional recovery from a car accident take?

There's no fixed timeline. Some people feel steadier within a few weeks; others carry anxiety, hypervigilance, or low mood for months, especially without support. Working with a trauma-informed counsellor typically helps shorten that timeline and makes the process feel less isolating.

When should I see a counsellor after a car accident?

If you're still feeling anxious, on edge, avoidant, or not like yourself two to three weeks after the accident, or if it's affecting your work, relationships, or driving, that's a good sign it's time to reach out. You don't need to wait for things to feel unbearable first.
