When Is It Safe to Drive Again
After a Crash?

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍️ Dave Dyck, MC, CCC 📍 Nanaimo, BC

The question isn't really whether your car is repaired. It's whether you can trust yourself behind the wheel again. Here's a compassionate, honest look at driving anxiety after an accident — and how to know when you're ready.

After a car accident, most people expect the big question to be about the vehicle. Is it repaired? Is it back from the shop? What's it going to cost?

But the question that actually keeps people up at night is different. It's quieter, and it's harder to say out loud: Can I trust myself behind the wheel again?

If that question feels familiar, I want you to know two things. First, you're far from alone — this is one of the most common things I hear from clients across Nanaimo and Vancouver Island. Second, feeling this way doesn't mean something is wrong with you. It means your nervous system is doing exactly what it's designed to do.

A lot of people feel embarrassed about their fear of driving. They tell themselves they should just "get over it," white-knuckle their way through it, or avoid the subject entirely. But driving anxiety after a crash isn't a character flaw — it's an understandable, common response to trauma. The goal isn't to force yourself back behind the wheel through sheer willpower. The goal is helping your nervous system learn, gradually and safely, that driving is safe again.

Why Driving Feels Different After an Accident

Your brain's number one job is keeping you alive. After a traumatic event like a car accident, it takes that job even more seriously — becoming far more alert to anything that might signal danger, especially anything connected to the accident itself.

This heightened alertness is called hypervigilance, and it's part of a broader set of trauma responses that include fight, flight, and freeze. Your nervous system essentially recalibrates itself around one priority: don't let that happen again. The trouble is, it often overcorrects, treating ordinary driving situations as though they carry the same danger as the moment of impact.

In practical terms, this can look like:

  • A racing heart or tight chest approaching an intersection, especially one that resembles where the accident happened
  • Sudden fear on highways, even ones you've driven for years
  • Tension or dread when it starts to rain
  • A spike of fear making left turns, particularly across oncoming traffic
  • Panic when another vehicle approaches quickly, even if it isn't actually close to you
  • Intrusive memories or flashbacks of the accident popping up while you drive
  • An exaggerated startle response to horns, sudden braking, or sirens

None of this means you're overreacting. It means your brain filed the accident as dangerous, and it's now doing its best to prevent a repeat — even in situations that are, in fact, perfectly safe.

Why This Doesn't Mean You're Weak

I want to be direct about this: fear of driving after an accident has nothing to do with courage, and everything to do with how trauma changes the way your brain processes danger.

Trauma doesn't just live in your memory — it lives in your body. The part of your brain responsible for detecting threats, the amygdala, can become more reactive after a traumatic event, while the part responsible for reasoning things through calmly can become temporarily less accessible under stress. This isn't a personal failing. It's neurology.

You might recognize this in small, everyday moments — flinching at a car door slamming nearby, feeling your stomach drop when someone brakes hard in front of you, or needing a minute to collect yourself before pulling out of the driveway. These reactions are your nervous system trying to protect you, even in situations where it no longer needs to.

Signs You May Not Be Ready Yet

There's no single test for "readiness" to drive again, but there are patterns that often suggest your nervous system is still working through what happened:

  • Panic or intense anxiety while actually driving
  • Avoiding certain roads, intersections, or routes altogether
  • White-knuckle driving — gripping the wheel tightly, rigid posture, shallow breathing
  • Constant, anxious mirror checking
  • Needing someone in the passenger seat to feel safe enough to drive
  • Feeling exhausted after even short drives
  • Driving noticeably slower than you used to, out of fear rather than caution
  • Avoiding highways entirely
  • Turning around or rerouting to avoid busy intersections
  • Feeling overwhelmed or dreading the drive before you've even left the house

If several of these sound familiar, that's useful information — not a verdict. It simply tells you where your nervous system currently is, and what kind of support might help you move forward.

Is It Better to Push Through It?

A common instinct is to force yourself back onto the road as quickly as possible, on the theory that the fear will fade if you just push through. Sometimes that works. Often, especially with a significant accident, it backfires — flooding an already overwhelmed nervous system and reinforcing the belief that driving really is dangerous.

On the other hand, avoiding driving altogether tends to make anxiety worse over time, not better. Every road you avoid, every route you reroute around, quietly confirms to your brain that those situations are, in fact, threatening — which only strengthens the fear.

The middle path — and the one that tends to work best — is gradual exposure. Rather than an all-or-nothing approach, this means taking small, manageable steps: starting with short, familiar drives, and slowly building up to more challenging situations like highways or busy intersections, at a pace your nervous system can actually tolerate. Each small success teaches your brain, bit by bit, that driving is safe — which is a very different process than simply telling yourself it's fine.

How Counselling Can Help

When clients come to me for support with driving anxiety, we don't start with the hardest road first. The process is paced, structured, and genuinely tailored to you. I generally begin with an assessment to understand how the accident has affected your thoughts, emotions, body, relationships, and day-to-day life — driving anxiety rarely shows up in isolation.

From there, we work on grounding skills and nervous system regulation — practical tools that help your body recognize safety in the moment, before we move into any deeper, trauma-informed processing of the accident itself. This sequencing matters. Trying to process a traumatic memory before your nervous system feels stable enough often makes things harder, not easier.

I also draw on CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy) to help identify and gently challenge the unhelpful thought patterns that often develop after an accident — beliefs like "I'm going to crash again" or "driving will never feel safe." These thoughts feel true in the moment, but they aren't the same as fact, and CBT gives us a structured way to work with them.

Here's what I want to be clear about: the goal isn't to convince you that nothing bad can ever happen. That wouldn't be honest, and it wouldn't actually help. The real goal is helping you develop genuine confidence that you can respond effectively if something does happen — while allowing your nervous system to stop living in constant survival mode. If your accident involved a concussion or other injury, this work often overlaps with trauma counselling in Nanaimo more broadly, since the two frequently go hand in hand.

Over time, most clients gradually regain their confidence, their independence, and a sense of calm behind the wheel — not by forcing it, but by giving their nervous system the support and pacing it actually needs. Practical Things You Can Start Today

Alongside counselling, there are small steps you can begin practicing on your own:

  • Breathe before you drive — a few slow, deliberate breaths before starting the car can help settle your nervous system before you even pull out of the driveway
  • Start with familiar roads — build confidence on routes you know well before tackling new or high-stress ones
  • Avoid rushing — give yourself extra time so you're not adding time pressure on top of anxiety
  • Take breaks — on longer drives, a short stop can help reset an activated nervous system
  • Celebrate small wins — a short, calm drive is real progress, even if it doesn't feel dramatic
  • Reduce self-criticism — being hard on yourself for feeling anxious tends to add more stress, not less
  • Practice grounding exercises — noticing five things you can see, or feeling your hands on the wheel, can help bring you back to the present moment
  • Aim for progress, not perfection — healing isn't linear, and a harder day doesn't erase the progress you've already made

When to Seek Help

It's worth reaching out for support if:

  • Driving anxiety has lasted weeks or months without easing
  • Your daily life is becoming more restricted because of avoidance
  • The anxiety is getting worse over time rather than better
  • You're experiencing panic attacks while driving or even thinking about it
  • It's starting to affect your relationships or your ability to work

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does driving anxiety last?

There's no fixed timeline. For some people it eases within a few weeks; for others, especially without support, it can persist for months or longer. The Canadian Mental Health Association notes that anxiety responses vary widely from person to person, and the right support tends to shorten that timeline considerably.

Can PTSD make driving difficult?

Yes. PTSD after an accident often includes hypervigilance, intrusive memories, and a strong startle response — all of which can make driving feel unsafe even when, objectively, it is.

Should I force myself to drive?

Not all at once. Forcing yourself into the hardest driving situations right away often backfires and can reinforce the fear. Gradual, paced exposure tends to work far better than pushing through everything at once.

Can counselling help me drive again?

Yes. Trauma-informed counselling, nervous system regulation, and CBT can help you understand what's happening in your body and gradually rebuild real confidence behind the wheel.

Will ICBC pay for counselling?

Often, yes. Under ICBC's Enhanced Care model, many people are entitled to pre-approved counselling sessions after a motor vehicle accident, regardless of fault. See does ICBC cover counselling? for the details, or visit ICBC directly to learn more about your claim.
